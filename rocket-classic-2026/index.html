The 60-Second Read

  • This is a new golf course. A $16.1M Tyler Rae restoration rebuilt every green to Ross's original contours, deepened the bunkers, removed the only water hazard and dropped par from 72 to 70. It debuts this week.
  • The whole change lives on two holes. 7 and 17 were par 5s averaging about half a stroke under par with 50%+ birdie rates. They are now 505- and 537-yard par 4s. Same scores against the new par would read +0.53 and +0.50.
  • Length barely moved — 7,358 to 7,328 yards. The difficulty is par, greens and fescue, not yardage.
  • Detroit used to be a birdie-fest, fingerprinting closest to TPC Twin Cities and TPC Deere Run. The restoration is explicitly built to end that.
  • Model says: Cameron Young, Si Woo Kim, Russell Henley head the structural venue fit.

The Restoration — What Actually Changed

Architect Tyler Rae, with NMP Golf Construction, completed a $16.1 million restoration of the 1920s Donald Ross design in the 2025–26 offseason. It debuts this week. This is not a touch-up: every green was rebuilt, the par dropped by two, and the course's only water hazard was filled in.

Greens

All 18 rebuilt to Donald Ross's original contours — raised and expanded, bentgrass, running to roughly 12½ on the Stimpmeter.

Par 72 → 70

The short par 5s at 7 and 17 became long par 4s. Only 4 and 14 remain par 5s. Twelve par 4s now, four of them over 500 yards.

Bunkers

Deepened and repositioned to be back in play for modern driving distance, realigned toward Ross's original angles.

Water gone

The pond fronting the par-5 14th was removed and replaced with a tall-fescue waste area. The course now has no water hazard.

Ditches

Deep cross-ditches restored — some reaching 18 feet — serving both strategy and the drainage problem that motivated the project.

Fescue & trees

Wispy fescue extended throughout, numerous trees removed, fairways refreshed and new tee boxes installed.

The two holes that carry the whole change

Holes 7 and 17 were two of the softest holes on the PGA Tour — both averaged roughly half a stroke under par with birdie rates above 50%. They are now 505- and 537-yard par 4s. Re-basing the exact same historical scores against the new par shows what the field is walking into:

Based on 3248 and 3247 played holes, 2019–2025. The re-based column is arithmetic on real scores, not a projection — but it assumes players attack the holes the same way, which they will not. Expect the true figure to land a little kinder as the field lays back off tees that no longer reward a go-for-the-green second.

What this means for every number in this document. Two things moved: hole geometry, and the green complexes. Geometry changed by a measurable amount per hole, so approach-distance reads can be graded hole by hole. The greens changed everywhere — rebuilt, raised, expanded and quicker. So even on a hole whose yardage barely moved, the approach-distance mix carries but the scoring, around-green and putting history does not. Nothing in this document treats "same yardage" as "same hole".

The signature is unmistakable and it is the birdie-fest profile: greens hit in regulation at the 91th percentile (72.5% vs a 67.1% Tour norm) and fairways found at the 71th. Players simply were not punished here. Nothing separated the field much more than average either — no single skill was the price of admission.

This is the profile the restoration was built to destroy. Deeper bunkers, fescue, rebuilt greens at ~12½ and two par 5s converted to 500-yard par 4s all attack the same thing: how easy it was to hit greens and make birdies. Expect GIR and fairway rates to fall and the separation numbers to rise. Treat this chart as the baseline being moved away from, not a forecast.

Babe's Babes — Putting & the Putting Trap PRE-RENO

Putting tracked finish at r=-0.52 — against a Tour-wide average of -0.51. That is essentially the tour norm, which means it is noise, not Detroit's edge.

The tempting (wrong) read: putting correlated with finish, so back the hot putter. That is the putting trap. Putting correlates with finish at nearly every event — it is noisy, and making putts is simply how scoring happens. The only informative question is whether it mattered more here than elsewhere, and at Detroit it did not: -0.018 versus the Tour mean, ranking 13 of 35. Middle of the pack.

What the restoration does to this. This is the one read most likely to change. Greens were rebuilt to Ross's original contours, raised, expanded, and are running near 12½ — more slope, more speed and more pin positions than the greens these correlations were measured on. If any number in this document moves, it is this one, and it moves upward. That is a reason to respect putting this week, but it is a structural argument, not something the history supports.

Every archived pin position at Detroit, k-means clustered per hole into zones, with how each zone actually played. Zone labels are computed on the approach axis — tee→green on par 3s, fairway→green elsewhere — so "back" means back from the player's view, not from an arbitrary map orientation.

Read this as geography, not as a scoring forecast. The greens were rebuilt to Ross's original contours — raised, expanded and quicker. What survives well is where a pin can be cut and the shape of each green's front/back/side geography, which the restoration largely restored rather than invented. What does not survive is the scoring attached to each zone: those numbers were set on surfaces that no longer exist. A back-left pin being historically the hardest on a hole is a useful prior about the hole's geometry; the exact stroke penalty is not.
